Sometimes the deliverable is not a machined part — it is a fabric product a
manufacturer has never seen, made real enough to be quoted.

## The problem

The client had a sketch: a pouch, 13 × 9 in, in leather or heavy fabric, with
two flexible hooks over a standard headboard, holding a book and a gooseneck
reading light.

The engineering questions hide behind the softness:

- **Hooks over an unknown headboard.** Headboards vary in thickness, so the
  hooks have to flex without losing grip or marking the wood.
- **The load is off-centre.** A book sits away from the hook line, so the pouch
  wants to rotate and pull away from the headboard.
- **The light moved mid-project** — originally on the side, then requested over
  the top — which changes where its mass and its cable go.
- **Two-tone colour** was requested for presentation, meaning the model had to
  carry material separation, not just shape.

## The approach

- **Panels laid out as a maker would cut them**, so the model translates into
  pattern pieces rather than into a surface no one can sew.
- **Hook flexibility treated as a material property** — the grip comes from the
  hook section being compliant across the range of headboard thicknesses.
- **Light mount relocated to the top edge** as requested, with the mass placed
  close to the hook line to reduce the rotating moment on the pouch.
- **Two-tone materials modelled properly**, so renders show the real seam and
  colour split instead of a painted-on effect.

## The result

A finished 3D model and presentation renders across four iterations, ready to
show to a manufacturer or to buyers.

## Where this applies

Soft goods that need engineering attention: bags, covers, holders, organisers.
Someone still has to decide how it hangs, where the load goes and how it is
cut.
