Press die components in an aluminium extrusion and metal forming operation kept
cracking in service. Each failure meant an unplanned line stop and another
tooling bill.

## The problem

Fatigue cracking is rarely a material problem and almost never random. It
repeats in the same place because the geometry puts it there. But on the shop
floor the symptom looks like bad luck: a tool that "just doesn't last", replaced
on a schedule nobody chose.

The real costs were the ones not on the tooling invoice — unplanned stops,
scrapped parts around the failure, and maintenance intervals set by fear rather
than by data.

## The approach

- **FEA on the critical components** (Ansys) — stamping die and bending tool
  parts loaded as they actually see service, not as a convenient simplification.
- **Located the stress concentrations** driving crack initiation, and separated
  the ones caused by geometry from those caused by the material and
  heat-treatment specification.
- **Redesigned** the geometry at those points and revised the material and
  heat-treatment spec accordingly.
- **Validated with follow-up analysis** before cutting metal, so the redesign
  was checked against the same load case that broke the original.

## The result

- **Fatigue-related tooling failures down 80%.**
- Maintenance intervals extended and, more importantly, based on evidence.
- The validated methodology was reused across the rest of the die portfolio —
  the second and third tools cost far less to fix than the first.

## Where this applies

If a tool fails in the same place every time, that is not wear — it is a design
input you can still change. An FEA study on one component usually pays for
itself in a single avoided line stop.
