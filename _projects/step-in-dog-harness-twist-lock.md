A design-led pet brand preparing to launch needed its first product engineered
properly: a step-in harness closed by a proprietary twist-lock dial, plus a
matching leash.

## The problem

The dial is the product. Everything else follows from whether it works:

- **It has to tension and hold** against a pulling animal, then release
  instantly when the owner wants it off.
- **It cannot be a cable tensioner borrowed from a boot.** Adapting the
  principle for a pet means different loads, different hygiene, and a user
  operating it one-handed on a moving target.
- **The harness is soft goods; the mechanism is moulded plastic.** The two have
  to be joined so the load path passes through webbing and stitching, not
  through a plastic boss.
- The founder also needed **renderings and functional animation for investors**,
  not just files for a factory.

## The approach

- **Mechanism first, garment second.** The closure was designed as a device with
  its own tolerances and duty, then integrated into the harness architecture.
- **Load into textile, not into plastic.** Anchor points carry force through
  reinforced webbing junctions; the moulded parts locate and lock rather than
  taking tension directly.
- **Designed for the DFM conversation.** The client expected a manufacturer to
  come back with revision requests, so parts were built parametrically to absorb
  moulder feedback without a restart.
- **One model, two outputs.** The same assembly produced the engineering
  drawings and the presentation renders, so what investors saw was the product
  that would actually be made.

## The result

A manufacture-ready design for the harness and its closure across four
iterations, with injection-moulding-ready parts, technical drawings and render
assets.

## Where this applies

Any product whose selling point is one custom mechanism. Get that part right —
loads, tolerances, one-handed operation — and the rest of the product is
detailing.
