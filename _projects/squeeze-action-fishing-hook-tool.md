The client already sold this product. It worked — you pushed a plug into a
sleeve with your thumb to trap the line and secure the hook. It just was not
pleasant to use, and it did not look like a finished tool.

## The problem

Converting a thumb push into a squeeze changes the whole force path:

- **The motion has to be redirected.** A squeeze is a rotation about a pivot; the
  plug needs linear travel. Something has to convert one into the other in a
  body small enough to hold.
- **The numbers are modest and specific**: about 13 mm of travel, no more than
  about 7 lb of insertion force. That is a gift — it means the mechanism can be
  light, and over-engineering it would only make the product worse.
- **The hook holder component has to be carried inside** the new body without
  losing the ability to load hooks.
- **It has to look better**, which was an explicit goal alongside the
  ergonomics.

## The approach

- **Stapler kinematics as the reference frame**, since a staple gun solves the
  same problem — hand squeeze in, controlled linear stroke out — and users
  already understand the gesture.
- **Sized to the real load.** With a ~7 lb requirement, link sections and pivots
  were kept slim; the mechanism was designed for the force that exists, not for
  a safety factor pulled from habit.
- **Several concepts, not one.** The client asked for a couple of approaches to
  how the mechanism could work, so alternatives were developed to be compared
  rather than a single answer presented as final.
- **Hook cartridge integration** treated as the constraint that positions
  everything else in the body.

## The result

A mechanism redesign taken through two iterations, with renders and CAD for a
product already in the market.

## Where this applies

Redesigning something that already sells. The risk is losing what works while
fixing what does not — so the existing function gets pinned down first, and only
the interaction changes.
