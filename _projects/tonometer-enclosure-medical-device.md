A tonometer measures the pressure inside a patient's eye. The client had the
sensing hardware working and needed it to become a device someone could hold and
use.

## The problem

Medical prototypes carry constraints that consumer products do not:

- **The transducer position is clinical, not stylistic.** Where it sits relative
  to the user's hand determines whether a reading is repeatable.
- **Everything inside was already specified** — component dimensions, a
  connection diagram showing how the parts wire together, and an existing outer
  shell that had to be improved rather than replaced wholesale.
- **The deliverable was a 3D-printable shell**, so wall sections, clearances and
  fastening all had to suit additive manufacturing at prototype quantities.

Mid-project the client revised the transducer geometry — the part on top turned
out to be a different size than first supplied.

## The approach

- **Model from the supplied data, not from the render.** Component envelopes and
  the connection diagram were treated as the source of truth, with the existing
  STL and SolidWorks files used as reference for intent.
- **Cable routing designed in.** The connection diagram was used to keep wire
  paths short and untangled, because a prototype that fights its own loom is
  slow to assemble and easy to damage.
- **Absorb the revision cleanly.** When the transducer changed, only the
  interface features needed to move — the rest of the shell held.
- **User-facing geometry checked against market references** the client supplied,
  so the device felt like an instrument rather than a printed box.

## The result

A complete 3D-printable enclosure housing the transducer, electronics and
battery, delivered through the transducer revision and ready for prototype
assembly.

## Where this applies

Medical and instrumentation prototypes where the sensing hardware exists and
the device around it does not. Send the connection diagram and the component
dimensions — those two documents remove most of the guesswork.
